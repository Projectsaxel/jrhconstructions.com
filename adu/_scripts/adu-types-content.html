Detached, Attached, or Garage Conversion: Which ADU Type Is Best for Your Massachusetts Home?

Every ADU type trades cost against privacy, rental income against disruption, and square footage against your lot's constraints. Here is how the four types compare in Massachusetts, with the numbers and the decision rule that picks the right one for your property and your goal.

The Four ADU Types and the One-Sentence Decision Rule

Massachusetts law recognizes one accessory dwelling unit by right regardless of form, so the choice among types is yours, not the town's. The four forms: a detached ADU (a separate backyard structure), an attached ADU (an addition sharing a wall with the main house), a garage conversion (transforming or building above an existing garage), and an internal conversion (carving the unit out of a basement or attic). All count against the same 900 square foot or 50% of gross floor area cap, and all must meet the full Massachusetts State Building Code as dwellings.

The decision rule that resolves most cases in one pass: maximum rental income and privacy points to detached; maximum budget efficiency points to conversion; family proximity with independence points to attached. The rest of this guide pressure-tests that rule against cost, lot constraints, and the specifics of Massachusetts housing stock, because the exceptions are exactly where expensive mistakes live.

Detached ADU: The Backyard Cottage

What It Is and What It Costs

A detached ADU is a freestanding small home: foundation, four walls, roof, full kitchen and bath, its own entrance and address feel. In Massachusetts, expect roughly $250,000 to $400,000+ all-in for site-built construction, with site work and utility trenching ($40,000 to $100,000 of that range) as the swing factor no catalog price includes. Prefab and modular units compress the construction phase but not the site costs.

Why It Wins on Income and Value

Detached units command the highest rents of any ADU type because they live like small houses: no shared walls, private entrance, often a private yard corner. For the same reason they appraise strongest and give owners the most flexibility across uses over time: tenant today, aging parent in five years, downsize-and-rent-the-main-house in fifteen. Under Massachusetts' by-right law there is no owner-occupancy requirement, so the detached unit is also the cleanest configuration for a pure investment play.

Where It Loses

It is the most expensive type per project and the most exposed to lot constraints: setbacks shrink the buildable area, lot coverage limits bite, trenching distance drives cost, and on septic properties the added bedroom math hits hardest. Detached is also the slowest path: full foundation, full weather exposure during framing, and in Massachusetts a construction calendar that respects winter.

Attached ADU: The Addition With Its Own Front Door

What It Is and What It Costs

An attached ADU extends the main house with a self-contained unit: separate entrance, full kitchen and bath, and a code-required fire separation between dwellings. Typical Massachusetts range: $180,000 to $300,000, cheaper than detached because it borrows the existing structure, foundation on one side, and short utility runs from the main systems.

The Family Proximity Sweet Spot

Attached is the configuration built for the multigenerational use case that drives a large share of Massachusetts ADU demand: aging parents or adult children close enough for daily contact, separate enough for dignity on both sides. A connecting interior door (lockable, code-compliant) is the detail that makes future caregiving practical without sacrificing present independence.

The Trade-Offs

Shared structure means shared acoustics, so the wall assembly deserves real soundproofing budget, and rental privacy never quite matches detached, which shows up in rent. Construction disrupts the main house more than a backyard build. And architecturally, a bad addition is visible forever: attached ADUs reward design quality, because the unit and the house must read as one building. On the legal side, note that the state's parking maximum of one space and the size cap apply identically, so attached offers no regulatory shortcut, only a financial one.

Garage Conversion ADU: The Budget Champion With Two Variants

Variant 1: Converting the Garage Itself

A garage conversion turns an existing detached or attached garage into a dwelling. Because the shell, slab, and roof already exist, it is usually the cheapest path to a full ADU: typically $120,000 to $220,000 in Massachusetts. The money goes where the garage was never designed to perform: insulating to the Stretch Energy Code (garage walls and slabs are thermally naked), raising or reworking the slab for plumbing and floor height, replacing the garage door wall with framed, windowed wall, and running utilities for kitchen and bath.

Two Massachusetts-specific checks decide feasibility fast. First, the slab and frost line: older garage slabs without proper footings may need structural work to support a heated dwelling through freeze-thaw cycles. Second, zoning position: many garages sit closer to lot lines than today's setbacks would allow for a dwelling, and converting a lawful nonconforming structure in place is usually approvable but requires the building department's interpretation in writing before design money is spent.

Variant 2: Building Above the Garage

The above-garage ADU adds a second story over an existing garage, preserving parking below. It prices closer to detached construction, roughly $200,000 to $320,000, because the existing garage structure rarely carries a dwelling load without structural reinforcement, and stairs, often exterior, consume budget and square footage. Its advantages are real: zero lost parking (worth noting even though the state caps what towns can require), excellent privacy from elevation, and no new lot coverage, which matters on lots already at their impervious limit.

Internal Conversions: Basement and Attic ADUs

The Cheapest Square Footage, With Code as the Gatekeeper

An internal conversion carves the ADU out of existing finished or finishable space, most often the basement. At roughly $80,000 to $180,000, it is the lowest-cost ADU type in Massachusetts, and in a state full of full-footprint basements under colonials and capes, it is also the most widely available opportunity. The gatekeepers are code, not zoning: minimum ceiling heights, egress windows sized and positioned for bedroom escape, moisture control, and fire separation from the main dwelling. Attic conversions add the geometry problem of sloped ceilings against minimum height rules, plus stair code.

Basement units rent below grade in both senses: expect lower rent per square foot than any other type, offset by the lowest cost per square foot to create. The Massachusetts Factors That Tip the Decision

Winter Is a Line Item

Massachusetts construction lives with a real winter, and each ADU type wears it differently. Detached builds need frost-protected foundations (footings below the frost line, typically four feet), and pouring concrete or framing through January costs money in heating, tenting, and schedule slack. Conversions flip the equation: a basement or garage conversion happens largely inside existing shell, making it the natural winter project. Smart sequencing uses this: permit in fall, convert through winter, or break ground on a detached unit in early spring to close the shell before the snow returns.

The Stretch Energy Code Rewards Compact Forms

Most Massachusetts municipalities enforce the Stretch Energy Code, and a growing list has adopted the Specialized Opt-In Code, pushing new dwellings toward high insulation and electrification. This quietly favors attached and conversion types, which share thermal envelope with the main house, over detached units, whose four exposed walls and roof must meet the standard alone. On a detached project, budget for continuous exterior insulation, heat pump systems, and tight air sealing as the default, not the upgrade.

Your House Probably Votes for a Type

Massachusetts housing stock has patterns, and the patterns have ADU affinities. The full-basement colonial or cape offers internal conversion potential most of the country lacks. The postwar ranch on a quarter acre often pairs a wide shallow lot with a detached one-car garage: conversion or backyard cottage territory. The side-entrance garrison with attached two-car garage is the above-garage candidate. And older village-center homes on tight nonconforming lots frequently rule out new footprint entirely, leaving conversion as the only path. Walking the property with these patterns in mind usually shortens the type debate to minutes.

Septic, Sewer, and the Bedroom Budget

Repeating the equalizer because it overrides everything above: on a septic property, Title 5 bedroom capacity is the first number to check, and it constrains all four types identically. A lot with one spare bedroom of capacity supports a one-bedroom unit of any type or a studio with headroom; a lot with none supports a studio or a septic upgrade. Sewered lots skip this entirely, which is why the same homeowner goal produces different type answers in Arlington (sewer) and in Carlisle (septic).

The Two Most Expensive Type Mistakes

First: building the cheap type for an income goal. The basement unit that saves $150,000 upfront but rents $800 below the detached alternative surrenders the savings within the first decade and appraises softer at sale. Run the rent spread for your actual town before letting the budget pick the type. Second, the mirror image: building the expensive type for a temporary need. Housing a 24-year-old for two years does not require a $350,000 cottage; a garage conversion does that job and then becomes a rental. The type should serve the twenty-year plan for the property, not the two-year situation that prompted the idea.

When the Matrix Ties, Let the Lot Break It

If two types serve the goal equally, the cheaper tiebreaker is always the one your lot is already offering: the sound detached garage, the dry full-height basement, the wide side yard outside every setback. Fighting the lot, structurally reinforcing a weak garage, waterproofing a wet basement, trenching 150 feet past ledge, is where comparable projects diverge by $50,000 or more. A one-hour professional walkthrough that reads the lot against the matrix is the cheapest decision insurance available, and it is exactly what a feasibility assessment is for.

Frequently Asked Questions About ADU Types in Massachusetts

What are the types of ADUs?

Four forms: detached (freestanding backyard unit), attached (addition with its own entrance), garage conversion (within or above an existing garage), and internal conversion (basement or attic). Massachusetts law protects one ADU by right regardless of form, under the same size cap.

What is the cheapest type of ADU to build?

A basement conversion, typically $80,000 to $180,000 in Massachusetts, followed by garage conversions at $120,000 to $220,000. Both reuse existing shell, which is where the savings live.

Which ADU type adds the most property value?

Detached units appraise strongest because they live like small houses and rent highest. Above-garage units follow closely. Basement units add the least appraised value per dollar of rent they produce.

Which ADU type rents for the most?

Detached, then above-garage, then attached, then basement. The spread between detached and basement in many Massachusetts markets runs several hundred dollars per month for comparable bedroom counts, enough to repay the higher construction cost over a typical holding period.

Is a garage conversion a good ADU?

Often the best value in the lineup: existing shell, zero new lot coverage, strong privacy if the garage is detached. The feasibility checks are the slab and footings, insulation to the energy code, and the zoning status of a garage that sits inside today's setbacks.

Can I build an ADU above my garage?

Usually yes, if the structure can carry the load, which typically means structural reinforcement. Above-garage units preserve parking, gain privacy from elevation, and add no lot coverage, at a cost approaching detached construction.

Do all ADU types count the same under the 900 square foot rule?

Yes. The state cap of 900 square feet or 50% of the main home's gross floor area, whichever is smaller, applies to the unit regardless of form, and Title 5 septic math counts bedrooms identically across all types.

Which type is best for aging parents?

An attached single-level unit designed for aging in place: zero-step entry, wide doorways, curbless shower, and a lockable connecting door for future caregiving. A single-level detached unit is the close second when privacy matters more than proximity.

Which type is fastest to build?

Internal conversions finish in roughly 2 to 4 months of construction, garage conversions in 3 to 5, attached in 4 to 7, detached in 5 to 8. Conversions also suit winter construction, since the work happens inside existing shell.

What works best on a small lot?

Types that add no footprint: basement, garage conversion, or above-garage. They bypass setback and lot coverage limits entirely, which are what disqualify detached units on tight lots.

Does Massachusetts weather change the choice?

It changes the cost and calendar more than the choice itself: detached units carry frost-depth foundations and weather-exposed framing, while the Stretch Energy Code makes their four exposed walls the most expensive envelope to insulate. Conversions are the natural winter projects.

Can I start with a home office and make it a rental later?

Build and permit it as a full dwelling from day one, even if it serves as an office first. Retrofitting kitchen, bath, and code compliance into a non-dwelling structure later routinely costs more than the difference would have at construction.

The Type Is a Strategy, Not a Floor Plan

Detached, attached, conversion: the names describe buildings, but the choice is really about what the property should be doing in ten years. Income favors detached. Family favors attached. Budget favors the shell you already own. And in Massachusetts, two local facts discipline every preference: Title 5 counts bedrooms before you count anything else, and the lot you have is quietly recommending one of the four answers.

The homeowners who choose well do it in two steps: name the twenty-year goal honestly, then let a professional walkthrough confirm which type the lot, the septic, and the structures already support.
